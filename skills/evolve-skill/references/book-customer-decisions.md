# Reading customer behavior

Use these decisions inside the selected Evolve research, creative, storefront or iteration method. Start with its product facts, dated customer evidence and control. Use a book to suggest a competing explanation or a better observation. Only the current job's evidence can support a claim about its buyers.

## Find the reason behind an action

**If a theme comes from prompted answers**, find out what happened before the prompt. Ask for the last occasion, what the person tried, the cost or inconvenience, and what they did next. Reflect their account and invite correction. Record the unprompted remark and the prompted answer separately. If someone agrees with the interviewer's suggested reason but bought for a different reason, retain both pieces of evidence. Sources include Carnegie on listening and Voss and Raz on summaries and calibrated questions.

**If successful buyers and nonbuyers describe the same desire**, compare the tasks they faced at the decision point. Pick cases with comparable access, experience and offers. A repeat buyer who already knows their size has an advantage a new visitor lacks. If the new visitor can explain the product but cannot select a fit, test size guidance. If both groups can select confidently and question performance, test a relevant demonstration instead. The comparison draws on the Heaths' treatment of successful exceptions in *Switch* and Gladwell's discussion of opportunity in *Outliers*.

**If group identity might matter**, ask which people the buyer actually trusts or wants to differ from. Look for a purchase, recommendation or rejection tied to that group. A named peer can reassure one audience and alienate another. When evidence conflicts, keep each buyer context attached to its observation. A demographic label, gesture or an image of a crowd cannot establish a motive. See Berger's *Invisible Influence*, chapters 2–3.

## Separate attention, recall and buying intent

If an ad gets more attention, work forward from the opening. What did the person notice? Can they name the advertised product, explain the benefit, and inspect the evidence for it? Which of those steps is measured? A visual surprise can improve views while attracting people with little need for the product. A useful tip can earn shares even when the product is incidental. Keep those as separate hypotheses. See Cialdini, *Pre-Suasion*, chapters 3–4, Berger, *Contagious*, chapters on triggers and stories, and the Heaths, *Made to Stick*, chapter on credibility.

For example, suppose a folding lunch container ad earns extra views when it opens on a collapsing object. A viable variant shows the container folding into a packed work bag, using verified dimensions. Hold the original offer, audience and placement steady. Compare purchases per comparable exposure or assigned visitor. Use attention and product-page visits to locate any change along the way. If traffic is too small for a purchase comparison, a comprehension check can tell you whether viewers understood the demonstration. Leave the sales effect open. In a reference-based static, make this change in the existing template's demonstration slot and preserve the surrounding copy roles.

## Make the page decision easier to inspect

Identify the specific decision a shopper struggles to make. For variants that look alike, compare each on the attribute that changes suitability, such as compatible model, fit or capacity. Show total price, any difference in quantity and recurring terms at selection. If shoppers need broad choice and find the exact SKU easily, preserve that route. If shoppers stall while comparing, test a quick fit guide or side-by-side comparison while keeping the same assortment. A real price mismatch warrants a repair before a persuasion experiment. See Schwartz, *The Paradox of Choice*, chapters 4 and 6, Ariely, *Predictably Irrational*, chapter 1, and Thaler and Sunstein, *Nudge*, chapters 5, 7 and 8.

Keep the denominator, assignment and checkout window consistent. A default that increases subscription selection can also increase cancellations and refund contacts. Measure those by eligible cohort over a suitable renewal window. Treat repeat use as its own question. Use the *Hooked* supplemental workbook's trigger and action exercises only where customers have a recurring task and a reason to return.

## Interpret a result and hand it off

For a striking winner, record how many comparable variants ran, their exposure, and how the winner was selected. A creative chosen after its strongest day may fall back toward typical performance on its next run through regression to the mean. Inspect the distribution, repeat the comparison under similar conditions and state which result would change the recommendation. If only winners are available, ask for the unsuccessful variants or keep the apparent pattern provisional. Sources include Kahneman, *Thinking, Fast and Slow*, chapters 10, 17 and 23, and Dobelli's discussions of survivorship and confirmation bias.

In a team handoff, give the owner the observed behavior, the best rival explanation and the next observation that would distinguish them. Attach the source, metric definition and acceptance example. For example, an image brief about fit is ready to shoot when the producer can identify the exact size, product variant and comparison object. The finished brief can stay short. See Martell, *Buy Back Your Time*, chapter 7, and Wickman, *Traction*, the data component.
