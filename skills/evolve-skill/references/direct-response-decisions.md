# Choosing a direct-response message

Use this guide within the selected Evolve workflow when you need to choose a promise, opening or proof. The questions draw on Eugene M. Schwartz’s *Breakthrough Advertising*. Page numbers refer to the 209-page PDF edition. Bring the current product facts, customer evidence, tested control and offer terms into the decision. A historical ad illustrates a writing choice. Its results and claims belong to that advertiser.

When two approaches fit, use [Choosing a method](choosing-a-method.md) to decide which evidence distinguishes them.

## Find a technique by its name in the book

- **Intensification** (pp. 78–106) makes a desired outcome more concrete by showing another meaningful occasion, perspective or consequence.
- **Identification** (pp. 107–124) connects the product with a role the buyer recognizes or wants to inhabit.
- **Gradualization** (pp. 125–144) orders the argument from a premise the reader accepts to a conclusion the evidence can support.
- **Redefinition** (pp. 145–155) changes how a buyer evaluates a real objection, such as effort or price. Preserve the underlying cost or trade-off.
- **Mechanization** (pp. 156–162) explains how the product delivers its promise. Choose the level of detail that resolves the buyer’s doubt.
- **Concentration** (pp. 163–171) addresses a competing way of satisfying the same desire. Verify the comparison on both sides.
- **Camouflage** (pp. 172–180) studies how the surrounding publication affects credibility. In a sponsored placement, adapt the reading format and keep the advertiser apparent.

## Choose the next move

| Decision | Evidence to inspect | How it changes the work |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Which outcome should lead? (pp. 16–24) | Look for what buyers already pursue, how much it matters in the moment, how often the need recurs and how widely the evidence applies. Identify the one product action that can deliver the selected outcome. | A vivid complaint from one person can justify exploring a segment. Establish reach separately before making it the broad campaign promise. |
| Where should the opening begin? (pp. 24–45) | Find what this reader already recognizes about the problem, category and product, what they believe it can do, and what is still stopping the purchase. | A buyer waiting for the right size needs availability and terms. A buyer who knows the brand but doubts the fit needs a measurement or demonstration. Both may have visited the same product page. |
| Has the category worn out the promise? (pp. 46–56, 156–162) | Compare dated competitor promises and explanations. Treat the reader’s knowledge and the market’s exposure as separate observations. Check whether the proposed mechanism is actually different and demonstrable. | When competitors already give the same explanation, show a consequential detail the buyer can inspect. A louder claim or a newly coined name needs its own evidence. |
| Does the next beat add anything? (pp. 57–72, 78–106) | Hold one buying reason steady. Ask what the next line or image lets someone picture, understand or verify for the first time. | Show a different use occasion or the action that produces the result. Two synonyms for the same payoff are wording variants. A more dramatic promise changes the test. |
| What makes the claim believable to this reader? (pp. 74–77, 107–162) | Find a starting fact the audience accepts, the inference they would question and the evidence that connects them. Check any role or identity against actual audience language or behavior. | Lead a skeptical reader from an observable operation to its bounded result. Put proof at the first material doubt. If the claimed result still exceeds the evidence, narrow it. A practical limitation stays visible after reframing. |
| Why choose this over the buyer’s real alternative? (pp. 145–171) | Compare both choices on the same task and under comparable conditions. For an unusual price, distinguish the verified terms from any documented reason the seller can offer them. | State the demonstrated difference and who benefits. Leave unsupported superiority and invented markdown stories out of the ad. |
| Which presentation earns another moment of attention? (pp. 172–207) | Identify what the audience expects in this placement, where it can inspect proof, and who is visibly speaking. | Borrow a useful format such as a demonstration or field note. Preserve sponsor identity, permissions and material qualifiers. If the opening raises a question, pay it off in the ad or at the promised destination. |

Use the relevant row when a decision is contested. In the working brief, identify the input that decided it and the missing observation that could reverse it. Keep that reasoning out of finished customer-facing copy unless the assignment asks for commentary.
