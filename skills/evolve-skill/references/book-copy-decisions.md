# Choosing and reviewing ad copy

Start with the audience, angle, offer, proof and placement approved in the Evolve brief. Use the decision below that is holding up the work. Keep source notes with the brief so a reviewer can check a decision without adding commentary to the ad.

## Choose the first thing the reader needs to know

Identify one proposition the reader must accept to take the next action. Write down what the customer already believes, the first part they might reasonably question and what this brand can show them. Use a dated customer observation or a recorded buying objection when one exists. A story about another advertiser can suggest a test. It cannot establish this buyer's awareness or this product's performance.

| What the evidence shows | Opening to try | What would change the choice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Buyers already ask for a particular configuration, price or shipping date. | Open with the answer and its verified terms. Put the next eligibility or fit question nearby. | If the question comes only from existing owners, check it against the intended acquisition audience before using it for cold traffic. |
| Buyers want an outcome and have a specific reason to doubt the solution. | Start with the observable operation that resolves the doubt. Show the crucial step before extending the promise. | If the available asset shows only the product at rest, limit the claim to what is visible or request the missing demonstration. |
| Buyers describe a recurring situation but rarely name this category. | Begin at that moment. Use a short incident or problem lead when the product plays a necessary, understandable part in its resolution. | Remove the product from the story. If the story still delivers its entire payoff, find a closer connection or try a direct lead. |

These distinctions combine Robert Collier's discussion of the reader's current interests, the lead selection examples in *Great Leads*, the central buying belief in Evaldo Albuquerque's *The 16-Word Sales Letter*, and the awareness questions in [Choosing a direct-response message](direct-response-decisions.md).

## Connect the claim to something the buyer can inspect

- **An unfamiliar process.** Ask a product owner to show the step that supposedly creates the difference. Describe the step and its bounded consequence. Other products can share that step. Claims of exclusivity need a comparison that actually establishes it. See the brewery account in Claude Hopkins's *My Life in Advertising*.
- **A skeptical buyer.** Place proof at the first point where the reader has to take the brand's word for something consequential. For a guarantee, state which cases qualify, what the customer must do and how the remedy works. For a performance claim, check the relevant measurement and conditions. Repeating the same test as a quote, chart and caption still supplies one observation. This draws on John Bejakovic on proof, Kyle Milligan on presenting evidence and Joseph Sugarman on resolving objections.
- **A static, landing page or swipe adaptation.** Identify what each occupied slot does for the buyer. A headline can promise an outcome, a caption can explain the photograph, and a small line can limit the conditions. Replace each slot with verified material that does its particular job. An original benefit slot needs a supported benefit. Flavor can fill a sensory-benefit slot when taste is the chosen buying reason. Read the headline and caption against the actual crop at phone size. Check any promised result against what a viewer sees or can verify at the destination. See the caption discussions in David Ogilvy's *Ogilvy on Advertising* and Drew Eric Whitman's *Ca$hvertising*.
- **A script or native story.** Describe the initial expectation, the action that disrupts it and the next observable consequence. Replace a vague emotional judgment with a telling detail where there is one. Watch the cut between the action and the result with the sound off. If the sequence implies a larger or faster effect than the evidence supports, change the shot or narrow the result. Clearly identify an illustrative scene. This draws on Will Storr on perceived causality, Eddie Shleyner on showing a feeling and Eugene Schwartz on linking concrete images. A plot from Christopher Booker is optional when the existing material supports it.

## Review a specific failure and design the next test

Mark the first line or frame where understanding, belief or interest drops. When meaning is unclear, replace the ambiguous subject or connection. When belief drops, add the missing observation or narrow the claim. When interest drops, cut the repeated premise or move the useful detail earlier. Compare each repair to the original with the same reader and buying proposition in mind. This adapts the peer and CUB reviews in *Agora's Big Black Book*.

For example, consider an illustrative brief for a clip-on lamp. Buyers ask whether it will fit a bed frame. A verified jaw range can answer that question directly. A photograph of the lamp on a bedside table cannot demonstrate clamp fit. A shot of the measured frame and the jaw closing on its edge supplies that missing evidence for **that frame**. For a retest, change the opening between the fit measurement and the measured demonstration while holding the offer, audience and destination steady. Record the intended buying action, denominator and response window. A stronger opening-view rate with unchanged purchase results supports an attention finding. A purchase improvement needs comparable traffic and enough observations to interpret. Joe Karbo's order records and the split-run comparisons in John Caples's *Tested Advertising Methods* and Claude Hopkins's *Scientific Advertising* inform this check.
